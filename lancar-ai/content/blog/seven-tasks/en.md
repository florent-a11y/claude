Most talk about AI is about what it might do one day. This list is about what it does reliably now, in businesses of five to fifty people. For each task we note what the AI needs from you, because that is usually the part nobody mentions.

## 1. Answering the questions you get every day

Prices, opening hours, location, parking, payment methods. An assistant answers these in seconds, at any hour.

**What it needs:** one page with the correct answers. If your prices live in three places, fix that first.

## 2. Taking bookings and reservations

The assistant proposes free times, collects the details and confirms. Your staff receive a notification instead of a conversation to manage.

**What it needs:** a single calendar or sheet that is the truth about availability. Two calendars mean double bookings, with or without AI.

## 3. Replying in the customer's language

A guest writes in Mandarin, Korean or French and gets a natural reply. Your team reads a summary in Indonesian or English.

**What it needs:** almost nothing. It helps to give the proper names of your services once, so they are not translated oddly.

## 4. Following up

A reminder the day before an appointment. A thank-you and a review request after the visit. A polite nudge to the customer who asked for a quote and went quiet.

**What it needs:** a list of who to contact and when, and the customer's agreement to be contacted.

## 5. Reading receipts and invoices

A photo of a supplier invoice becomes a line in your purchases file: date, supplier, items, total.

**What it needs:** one habit. Every receipt goes to the same folder or chat group, and someone checks the totals once a week.

## 6. The daily recap for the owner

Sales, bookings, cash and open issues, in one message every morning.

**What it needs:** your sales data in a file or an export from your point-of-sale system. The recap can only be as good as what is recorded.

## 7. First drafts

Social media captions, replies to reviews, quotes, job adverts, staff instructions. The AI writes the first version; a person corrects and approves it.

**What it needs:** a few examples of how you write, and someone who reads before sending.

## What these tasks have in common

They are repetitive, they rely on information you already have, and a mistake is easy to spot and fix. That is the profile of a task worth handing over.

The opposite profile deserves caution: decisions about money, complaints, anything medical or legal, and any answer that would be costly if wrong and that nobody checks. Keep a person there.

## Where to start

Pick the one task that eats the most hours. Write down the information it needs. Try it for two weeks with a person checking every result. If it holds, move to the next one.
